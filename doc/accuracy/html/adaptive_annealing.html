Adaptive Annealing for Stein Variational Particle Filters:
A KL-Divergence Constrained Approach

Research Notes
Stochastic Volatility Modeling & High-Frequency Applications
February 2025

Abstract

We present an adaptive annealing scheme for Stein Variational Particle Filters (SVPF) that automatically adjusts the tempering schedule based on particle distribution statistics. Traditional fixed annealing schedules fail during extreme market events—such as flash crashes—where the likelihood landscape changes dramatically between timesteps. Our method uses KL-divergence constraints to determine step sizes, with safety interlocks for gradient magnitude and particle diversity. Empirical results on cryptocurrency data with 50σ volatility spikes show 50-90% reduction in estimation bias compared to fixed schedules, while maintaining computational tractability through single-sync GPU optimization.

1. Introduction

Particle filters approximate posterior distributions through a collection of weighted samples. In stochastic volatility models, these particles represent hypotheses about the latent log-volatility state $h_t$. The Stein Variational Particle Filter (SVPF) enhances traditional particle filters by using gradient information to transport particles toward high-probability regions, avoiding the weight degeneracy that plagues standard importance sampling approaches.

A critical component of SVPF is annealing—the gradual introduction of likelihood information to prevent particle collapse. Without annealing, a sharp likelihood function can cause all particles to collapse onto a single mode, destroying the diversity needed for uncertainty quantification. However, existing fixed annealing schedules are calibrated for "typical" market conditions and fail catastrophically during extreme events.

During flash crashes, the posterior shifts dramatically, requiring many more annealing stages to prevent particle collapse. Fixed schedules cannot adapt to these regime changes.

2. The Problem: Likelihood Shock

Consider a stochastic volatility model where returns $r_t$ follow:

$$r_t = \exp(h_t / 2) \cdot \varepsilon_t, \quad \varepsilon_t \sim t_\nu(0, 1)$$

The log-likelihood of observing return $r_t$ given log-volatility $h$ is:

$$\log p(r_t | h) = C - \frac{h}{2} - \frac{\nu + 1}{2} \log\left(1 + \frac{r_t^2}{\nu \cdot e^h}\right)$$

During normal markets, returns $|r_t|$ are small, and the likelihood function is relatively flat—particles distributed around the current volatility estimate all receive similar weights. However, during a flash crash with $|r_t| = 10\sigma$ or larger, the likelihood becomes extremely peaked: only particles at very high volatility receive non-negligible probability mass.

Problem: The Likelihood Shock

When the observation $r_t$ deviates significantly from model expectations, the likelihood function $p(r_t | h)$ concentrates sharply around a new mode far from the current particle locations. Direct application of this likelihood causes weight collapse—all probability mass concentrates on 1-2 particles, destroying the ensemble's ability to represent uncertainty.

2.1 The Annealing Solution

Annealing addresses weight collapse by introducing the likelihood gradually. Instead of the full posterior $p(h | r_t) \propto p(r_t | h) \cdot p(h)$, we construct a sequence of intermediate distributions:

$$\pi_\beta(h) \propto p(r_t | h)^\beta \cdot p(h), \quad \beta \in [0, 1]$$

At $\beta = 0$, we have the prior. At $\beta = 1$, we have the full posterior. By slowly increasing $\beta$ and allowing particles to equilibrate at each stage, we guide the ensemble from prior to posterior without catastrophic collapse.

Figure 2: Annealing bridges prior and posterior. The prior distribution (blue) is wide, centered at the previous volatility estimate. The posterior (red) is narrow and shifted due to the extreme observation. Annealing provides a smooth path for particles to migrate from prior to posterior without collapsing.

2.2 Why Fixed Schedules Fail

Traditional fixed annealing uses a predetermined schedule, typically 3-5 stages with values like $\beta \in \{0.3, 0.65, 1.0\}$. This works when:

  • The posterior is close to the prior (small innovation)
  • The likelihood is not sharply peaked
  • Particles have time to equilibrate between stages

During extreme events, none of these conditions hold. A 10σ return creates a likelihood that is essentially a delta function compared to the prior. Jumping from $\beta = 0.3$ to $\beta = 0.65$ can cause the effective sample size (ESS) to drop from 500 to 3, triggering immediate collapse.

3. The Solution: KL-Constrained Adaptive Annealing

Our approach determines the annealing step size $\Delta\beta$ adaptively at each stage based on the current particle distribution. The key insight comes from Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) theory: the KL-divergence between successive tempered distributions is approximately:

$$D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\beta + \Delta\beta} \| \pi_\beta) \approx \frac{1}{2} (\Delta\beta)^2 \cdot \text{Var}[\log p(r_t | h)]$$

This leads to a principled step size formula: to maintain a target KL-divergence $\tau$, we should use:

$$\Delta\beta = \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\text{Var}[\log p(r_t | h)]}}$$
Solution: Variance-Adaptive Step Sizing

High variance in log-likelihood (particles disagree) → small $\Delta\beta$ (move carefully)
Low variance in log-likelihood (particles agree) → large $\Delta\beta$ (move quickly)

This automatically allocates computational effort where it's needed: many small steps during extreme events, few large steps during calm periods.

3.1 Safety Interlocks

The variance-based step alone can fail in edge cases. We introduce three safety interlocks:

Safety Interlock 1: KL Constraint

$$\Delta\beta_{\text{KL}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\tau}{\text{Var}[\log p(r_t | h)] + \epsilon}}$$

Prevents distribution collapse by limiting how much the target can change in one step.

Safety Interlock 2: Gradient Constraint

$$\Delta\beta_{\text{grad}} = \frac{\kappa}{\mathbb{E}[|\nabla \log \pi_\beta|] + \epsilon}$$

Prevents particle explosion when gradients are large. If the Stein transport would move particles too far, we slow down.

Safety Interlock 3: Diversity Constraint

If $\text{Std}[h] < \theta_{\text{collapse}}$, force $\Delta\beta = \Delta\beta_{\text{min}}$

Detects mode collapse and forces tiny steps to allow particles to spread before continuing.

The final step size is the minimum of all three constraints, with hard clamps for stability:

$$\Delta\beta = \text{clamp}\left(\min(\Delta\beta_{\text{KL}}, \Delta\beta_{\text{grad}}, \Delta\beta_{\text{div}}), 0.01, 0.25\right)$$

4. Integration with SVPF

The Stein Variational Particle Filter combines importance sampling with deterministic transport. At each timestep, particles are updated through:

  1. Prediction: Propagate particles through the state transition model
  2. Annealing + Transport: Gradually introduce likelihood while moving particles via Stein gradient
  3. Output: Compute posterior statistics (mean, variance)

The Stein transport update for particle $h^{(i)}$ is:

$$h^{(i)} \leftarrow h^{(i)} + \epsilon \cdot \phi(h^{(i)})$$

where the Stein operator $\phi$ balances two forces:

$$\phi(h) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[k(h^{(j)}, h) \nabla_{h^{(j)}} \log \pi_\beta(h^{(j)}) + \nabla_{h^{(j)}} k(h^{(j)}, h)\right]$$
Figure 5: The two components of Stein transport. Attraction (left): particles are pulled toward high-probability regions via the score function gradient. Repulsion (right): the kernel gradient pushes particles apart to maintain diversity. The balance prevents collapse while ensuring convergence.

Adaptive annealing affects the gradient term through $\beta$: at low $\beta$, particles primarily respond to the prior (maintaining spread); at high $\beta$, the likelihood dominates (focusing on the observation). By controlling $\beta$ adaptively, we control the rate at which likelihood information enters the transport dynamics.

Key Finding: Bias Reduction

The primary benefit of adaptive annealing is bias reduction, not RMSE reduction. Fixed annealing systematically underestimates volatility during crashes (positive bias across all scenarios). Adaptive annealing centers the estimates, achieving near-zero bias even during 50σ events.

6. Computational Considerations

Adaptive annealing requires computing particle statistics (variance of log-weights, gradient magnitudes, particle spread) at each stage. A naive implementation incurs significant overhead from GPU-CPU synchronization. We employ two optimizations:

6.1 Single-Sync Architecture

Rather than synchronizing at every stage, we compute statistics once at $\beta = 0$ and estimate the number of stages upfront:

$$N_{\text{stages}} = \lceil 1 / \Delta\beta \rceil$$

This allows all subsequent stages to execute asynchronously without CPU involvement, reducing synchronization overhead from $O(N_{\text{stages}})$ to $O(1)$.

6.2 Fused Kernel Computation

Statistics computation is fused into the gradient kernel using warp-level primitives. After computing the gradient for each particle, threads cooperatively reduce statistics using shuffle instructions, avoiding separate kernel launches and memory round-trips.

7. Discussion

Adaptive annealing addresses a fundamental limitation of fixed tempering schedules: they cannot anticipate the difficulty of each filtering step. By using real-time particle statistics, the algorithm allocates computational resources where they are most needed—many careful steps during regime changes, few aggressive steps during stable periods.

The approach is particularly valuable for high-frequency financial applications where:

  • Extreme events are common: Cryptocurrency markets regularly experience 5-10σ moves
  • Bias is costly: Systematic volatility underestimation leads to mis-priced options and inadequate risk reserves
  • Latency matters: Decisions must be made in microseconds, not milliseconds

The single-sync optimization makes adaptive annealing practical for real-time applications, achieving the accuracy benefits without prohibitive computational overhead.

8. Conclusion

We presented an adaptive annealing scheme for Stein Variational Particle Filters that automatically adjusts tempering schedules based on particle distribution statistics. The method uses KL-divergence constraints with gradient and diversity safety interlocks to determine step sizes. Empirical results on extreme cryptocurrency scenarios demonstrate 50-90% bias reduction compared to fixed schedules, with the estimates correctly centered around true volatility even during 50σ flash crashes.

The approach integrates naturally with GPU-accelerated SVPF implementations through single-sync architecture and fused kernel computation, maintaining real-time performance while improving accuracy during the market conditions where it matters most.
