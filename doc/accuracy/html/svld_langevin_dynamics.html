SVLD: Stein Variational Langevin Dynamics

Preventing Variance Collapse in Stein Particle Filters
February 2025 · Research Notes

1. The Variance Collapse Problem

Deterministic SVGD transports particles toward the target posterior through a combination of attraction (gradient term) and repulsion (kernel gradient term). In theory, the repulsion should maintain particle diversity. In practice, Ba et al. (2021) showed that SVGD systematically underestimates posterior variance—particles cluster more tightly than they should.

Why Variance Collapse Occurs

The repulsion term $\nabla_x k(x, x')$ depends on inter-particle distances. As particles converge, they enter a region where the kernel gradient becomes small, reducing the repulsion force. The system reaches an equilibrium with less spread than the true posterior.

This is especially problematic for filtering applications where proper uncertainty quantification is essential for:

  • Calibrated confidence intervals
  • Risk management decisions
  • Downstream predictions
Figure 1: Variance collapse in deterministic SVGD. Particles start well-spread (left), but after SVGD iterations they cluster too tightly (center), underestimating the true posterior variance. SVLD maintains proper spread (right) through stochastic diffusion.

2. The SVLD Solution

SVLD converts the deterministic SVGD update into a stochastic differential equation by adding Langevin diffusion noise:

Deterministic SVGD
$$\frac{dh}{dt} = \phi(h)$$

Particles follow gradient flow deterministically. No randomness → eventual collapse.

SVLD (Langevin Dynamics)
$$dh = \phi(h) \, dt + \sqrt{2T} \, dW_t$$

Adds Brownian noise $dW_t$ scaled by temperature $T$. Maintains exploration.

In discrete time, the update becomes:

$$h_{t+1} = h_t + \epsilon \cdot \phi(h_t) + \sqrt{2 \epsilon T} \cdot \xi_t, \quad \xi_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$
Why This Works

The noise term $\sqrt{2T} \, dW_t$ provides continuous perturbation that prevents particles from settling into a collapsed configuration. The temperature $T$ controls the trade-off:

  • Higher T: More exploration, better coverage, but noisier estimates
  • Lower T: Less noise, tighter clustering, risk of under-coverage

In the limit as iterations → ∞, SVLD samples from the correct posterior distribution, while deterministic SVGD converges to a point mass approximation.

4. Temperature Selection

The temperature parameter $T$ is critical. Too low, and you get deterministic behavior with variance collapse. Too high, and particles become too noisy to provide useful estimates.

Figure 3: Temperature spectrum. The optimal value balances exploration (avoiding collapse) with exploitation (accurate tracking). For stochastic volatility, $T \approx 0.45$ works well empirically.

4.1 Implementation

In our SVPF implementation:

// Configuration
use_svld = 1;
temperature = 0.45f;  // Tuned for SV model

// Update step
h_new = h + step * φ(h) + sqrt(2 * step * T) * randn();

The noise is sampled fresh at each timestep using a fast GPU-friendly RNG (PCG32 or cuRAND).

5. SVLD in the Full SVPF Stack

SVLD complements the other techniques we've discussed:

The Complete Picture
Technique Problem Solved Mechanism
Adaptive Annealing Particle collapse during crashes KL-constrained β stepping
Newton Preconditioning Slow convergence Curvature-adapted step sizes
Full Newton Particles left behind Kernel-coupled Hessians
SVLD Variance collapse / under-coverage Langevin diffusion noise

Together, these form a robust filtering system: annealing prevents collapse during likelihood shocks, Newton methods ensure fast convergence, and SVLD maintains proper uncertainty quantification throughout.

6. When SVLD Matters Most

SVLD is most important when:

  • Uncertainty quantification is critical — risk management, option pricing, confidence intervals
  • Multi-modal posteriors — noise helps particles escape local modes
  • Long tracking sequences — variance collapse accumulates over time
  • Low particle counts — fewer particles collapse faster

For pure point estimation (just the mean), deterministic SVGD may suffice. But for any application requiring calibrated uncertainty, SVLD is essential.
